§1

The six findings that change the project

Ordered by how much they cost you if you don't act on them. Four are bad news. Two are the best news in the whole review.

01

In India, the word "screening" is what makes you a regulated medical device

Niva's positioning — "screening, explicitly not diagnostic" — has been treated as a shield. Under CDSCO's Medical Device Software guidance it is the opposite. The general-wellness exclusion carries an explicit carve-out: software intended to measure, estimate or report physiologic values for medical or clinical purposes, including screening, monitoring or alerting, is not general wellness software. Section 6 lists screening/triage as a medical purpose outright. Software that drives hardware inherits the hardware's risk class.

You have two honest options: drop every disease reference and claim activity/loading tracking only, or accept that Niva is a regulated device and plan accordingly. The current framing gets you the regulation without the protection.

CDSCO/MD/GD/MDSW/01/2026, issued 21 July 2026 · doc no. and scope independently corroborated (Nishith Desai / National Law Review, 3 Aug 2026)

02

Your FSR402s saturate at 20 N. Heel strike is 300–800 N.

The FSR402's nominal range is 0.2–20 N. Under sustained compressive load it saturated within 2–3 seconds on the first trial, took ~5 s and got noisier on the second, and by the third trial was completely saturated throughout — the authors attribute it to irreversible microstructural damage from cyclic loading.

Your four pressure channels are not measuring pressure. They are binary contact detectors that degrade over a few thousand steps. Everything downstream that treats them as a magnitude — time-pressure exposure, load asymmetry ratios, any kPa figure — inherits that.

Sêco et al. 2025, Sensors 25(23):7377 — verified directly against the paper

03

Four sensors is below the literature's own floor for centre of pressure

Benchmarked against a 99-sensor Pedar-X, layouts of 3–9 sensors produced 8–11 mm medial-lateral and 20–22 mm anterior-posterior CoP error, and the authors state plainly that caution is warranted at nine sensors or fewer. Thirteen is their recommended compromise. A separate systematic review of 33 insole studies puts the recommended minimum at fifteen.

Niva has four. Publishing a CoP trajectory from this build is not defensible. The one variable that survives sensor reduction is medial-lateral CoP — 0.61% RMSE even at 16 sensors, while ground reaction force in the same study hit 82.4%.

Fuchs et al. 2024, Sensors 24(15):4918 · Ciniglio et al. 2021, Sensors 21(4):1450 · Santos et al. 2024, Appl Sci 14:6085

04

Your EMA filter is erasing the heel-strike edge your piezo exists to catch

A single-pole EMA at α = 0.15 has a −3 dB cutoff of roughly 0.026 × fs and a DC group delay of (1−α)/α = 5.67 samples. At 100 Hz that is a 2.6 Hz cutoff and 57 ms of lag — half the lowest cutoff in common use, and a delay larger than the entire error budget of a good pressure-based event detector (1.0 ms in the best published algorithm).

Published practice is a zero-phase Butterworth low-pass at 5–15 Hz. Worse, the lag is frequency-dependent, so it distorts the intervals between events, not just their absolute timing — which corrupts exactly the asymmetry and phase-duration features you compute from them.

Derived from the filter definition; benchmarks from Lin et al. 2025 Sensors 25:3481 · Blades et al. 2023 Sensors 23:2736

05

The competing modality's best trial failed, and it failed at behaviour, not sensing

DIATEMP is the largest, best-designed RCT in diabetic foot monitoring — 304 patients, 18 months, home thermometry. It missed its primary endpoint: 29.1% vs 37.3% ulcer recurrence, RR 0.782, p = 0.133. Of 83 participants who detected a hotspot, only 24 actually reduced walking — and in those 24, RR was 0.336 (p = 0.017).

The signal was detected. The patients didn't act. Add Littman's 1,137-veteran study — 37.6% abandoned the leading commercial device within a year, with the sickest patients abandoning most — and the picture is consistent: detection is solved, acting on it is not. That gap, not better sensing, is the opportunity.

Bus et al. 2021, BMJ Open Diab Res Care 9:e002392 · Littman et al. 2024, JMIR Diabetes 9:e53083

06

Nobody has published this from a low- or middle-income country. Not one study.

A scoping review screened 1,925 papers on data-driven remote care for diabetic foot and included 81. It found zero studies from India or any low- or middle-income setting. Only 15 of the 81 had cohorts above 100 participants; only 7 were randomised.

This is your strongest honest claim, and it is a novelty claim rather than a clinical one: a competently run Indian pilot would be the first of its kind in the published record. It costs nothing to make and no competitor can take it from you.

Sharp et al. 2023, Front Clin Diabetes Healthc 4:1212182

§4

Hardware: what to change before the next board

Each sensor needs its own calibration curve

Across 64 FSR402 specimens, sensitivity varied with a coefficient of variation of roughly 26% (μ = 0.0735 V/N, σ = 0.019). A separate study varying temperature, sensor curvature and tissue compliance concluded directly that if multiple FSRs are used in a system, they must be calibrated independently.

This is a latent defect, not a refinement. Four sensors sharing one curve will encode inter-sensor manufacturing variation as left-right gait asymmetry — which is precisely the signal your heuristics are hunting for. You would be detecting your own bill of materials.

Calibration method is worth 20 percentage points, free

Full-range error by calibration method on a Tekscan sensor. Zero hardware cost — this is arithmetic and a loading protocol.

Calibrating on the loading curve alone is the worst case: including the unloading curve cut force estimation error by 76% in a hysteresis study that measured 7.6–17% hysteresis on FSRs against a manufacturer claim of under 4.5%.

Your one-shot baseline is calibrating the thing that drifts most

Across 24–60 °C, FSR sensitivity drift was ±6.7%/−6.1% — but null-force output drift was ±16.2%/−13.8%, and non-deterministic between temperatures. Averaging 100 stationary samples at boot fixes a zero that will have moved 20% by the time the shoe reaches foot temperature. Re-zero periodically during detected quiet standing instead.

The paper that is your architecture, one generation ahead

A 2026 paper built eight FSR402s on an ESP32-C3 with a 74HC4051 8:1 analog mux, BLE, and a 3D-printed 12.7 mm loading puck for calibration, at roughly USD 48 per pair. It reached 88% accuracy classifying diabetic vs non-diabetic plantar patterns and found forefoot pressure elevated 36–44% in diabetic participants.

Three things to copy immediately: the mux (eight channels off one ADC pin), the 1 kΩ divider resistor instead of your 10 kΩ — 1 kΩ pushes the sensitive region toward body-weight loads, which is the correct direction — and the matched-diameter loading puck.

If the flexible matrix gets built, build it capacitiveEvery failure mode hurting your FSRs is materially better in capacitive sensing at equal sensitivity: hysteresis ~2% vs "significantly different" loading/unloading curves, stable across 3,000 s cyclic testing vs visible drift, and stable from 25–60 °C where piezoresistive showed "extremely unstable" resistance change. A four-site BLE capacitive insole with a published parts list already exists — same sensor positions as yours, swapping FSR402 + divider for PDMS pads and an FDC1004.

Two hardware limits the firmware cannot work around

The MPU6050 caps at ±16 g, but foot heel-strike accelerations were measured at 24.6 ± 4.1 g. It will clip, and clipping biases everything downstream of integration — so impact magnitude must come from the piezo, never the accelerometer. Second, the medial arch is the penalised mounting position: ranked below insole and midsole-cavity placements, with occasional outliers above 30 cm attributed to foot interference. Rare large outliers are exactly what inflates a variance-based heuristic, which is what your ankle-sway metric is.

Two of your heuristics are contradicted outright

Impact peak as a risk score. Ground reaction force impact peak correlated with peak tibial bone load at r = −0.29 ± 0.37 — slightly negative — and 76 of 80 subject-specific correlations contradicted the assumption that higher impact means higher internal load. The authors named wearables as the field getting this wrong. Present the piezo as an event detector and gait-quality feature.

Medial-lateral asymmetry as the primary risk signal. The one prospective study with a full 250 Hz pressure plate and 155 subjects found the predictive signal was forefoot peak pressure — toes 2–5, metatarsals 1 and 3 — and reached only AUC 0.639 even so. Healthy people are also genuinely asymmetric: the dominant limb generates 56–61% of positive work, so any population-fixed threshold carries a built-in false-positive floor. Use Symmetry Angle rather than the classic Symmetry Index, which is undefined at y = −x and inflates when both signals sit near the noise floor; SA was the only index showing no speed dependence (p = 0.28).

§7

What the six-month plan should actually say

Your current plan has the right shape. The literature gives it a vocabulary regulators recognise, a validation protocol you can afford, and two steps you haven't budgeted.

Use the V3 framework's names

Verification (sensor output against specification, on the bench) → analytical validation (does the algorithm measure the intended physiological metric, in vivo) → clinical validation (does the measure identify a clinical state, in a defined population). Your months 1–2 are verification. Months 3–5 are analytical validation. The clinician-reviewed pilot is the start of clinical validation, not the whole of it. V3+ adds usability validation as a fourth component — worth taking seriously, since for an insole worn by people with reduced foot sensation, usability failure is the single most likely way the programme fails.

Write a one-sentence context of use before anything else. Everything downstream is judged against it.

Copy this validation protocol — it costs one action camera

A 2024 study validated a 12-sensor insole in 17 chronic stroke patients against frame-by-frame video labelling from a single GoPro at 120 Hz, dual-rater. It reported 90.86% of 3,820 gait events correctly identified, stance duration ICC 0.874, test-retest ICC 0.928. Comparable sensor count, n of 17, no motion-capture lab. This is a publishable analytical validation an undergraduate team can execute inside six months.

Two steps missing from your budget

Prospective CTRI registration — free, but must precede your first participant, and retrofitting it is not possible. And ethics committee registration: confirm your host institution's committee holds a current DHR/NECRBHR registration number and put it in the protocol. An unregistered committee's approval will not support publication or any later regulatory filing. ICMR's guidelines require EC review of all human research including healthy volunteers — but supervised walking in an insole is arguable as minimal risk, which is the specific claim that qualifies you for expedited rather than full-committee review.

Statistics that will survive a reviewer

Report Bland-Altman limits of agreement, not correlation — correlation is the wrong statistic for method comparison and it is the most common error in student validation work. Pre-declare a clinically acceptable difference before you collect data. And be honest about what a small n does: establishing agreement properly needed n = 83 in a worked example, so present your healthy-volunteer phase as estimating the mean and SD of differences to power a future study, not as establishing agreement. A pilot of 10–25 per group is defensible as a pilot when the protocol says its objective is feasibility and variance estimation.

One design decision to make now rather than later: if any output is model-derived, WHO's regulatory considerations call for external validation, which means holding out a cohort the model never sees. That has to be planned before data collection, not after.

§8

Ten things to do, in order

  1. Ask CDSCO for a written classification opinion

    Their own FAQ 78 provides for it: submit product documentation and request a classification from the Central Licensing Authority. If a foot-worn gait analyser lands Class A non-sterile non-measuring, the clinical-investigation chapter falls away and only free registration remains. This single letter determines your entire regulatory cost.

    Cost: a letter · Blocks: everything else
  2. Rewrite the claim, both ways, and pick one

    Draft the wellness version (activity and loading tracking, no disease reference) and the regulated version. You cannot have the screening language and the wellness exemption together.

    Cost: an afternoon
  3. Reply to the doctor's email thread

    Still the highest-value unactioned item in the project. Everything in this document is better with one clinician's reaction to it.

    Cost: one email
  4. Give each FSR its own calibration curve

    Ten-point cubic, loading and unloading, per sensor, using a matched-diameter loading puck. 24% error becomes 0.6%. Without this you are measuring your own component tolerances.

    Cost: zero BOM · Highest accuracy return available
  5. Replace the EMA with a Butterworth low-pass

    2nd-order at 10–15 Hz on FSR channels; leave the piezo peak path unsmoothed and burst-sample it at ≥500 Hz.

    Cost: firmware
  6. Run the StepUP-P150 subsampling experiment

    Answers "how many sensors and where" with 200,000 real footsteps before you spend on hardware.

    Cost: a weekend of Python
  7. Fatigue-test one FSR402 to 10,000 cycles

    Log sensitivity every 500 cycles under ~500 N. Given the published degradation across three trials, you will likely produce bad news — and that data is more valuable to your team than any accuracy claim, because nobody has published a dedicated in-shoe fatigue study.

    Cost: one sensor, one fixture
  8. Do the ISO 14971 hazard analysis

    False reassurance from a missed high-pressure region, sensor drift, battery and thermal risk against insensate feet. It is cheap, it is named in the CDSCO guidance, and it is the first document any reviewer or investor asks for.

    Cost: a week · Named in CDSCO guidance
  9. Retrieve the five blocked papers

    Pitei 1996 on FSR creep in an actual plantar insole is the most on-point missing source. Also Hall 2008 (the conditioning protocol you need before writing a calibration SOP), the IWGDF 2023 prevention guideline, the Crawford meta-analysis, and Waaijman 2013. All reachable via DELNET/INFLIBNET N-LIST or by emailing corresponding authors.

    Cost: library access
  10. Delete the synthetic pathology signatures from anything a clinician sees

    Keep them in the test harness. A demo showing simulated Parkinsonian and stroke gait in a product with no patient data is a credibility risk in exactly the room where credibility is the currency.

    Cost: a config flag

How this was built, and what to trust

Six parallel searches across PubMed/PMC, MDPI, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, Nature, Frontiers, arXiv, PhysioNet, UCI, CDSCO and ISO/IEC. Every citation here was located by search during compilation; none was written from memory. Where a paper's full text was reachable, the numbers quoted were read off the source.

Items marked unverified were located and their existence confirmed, but a specific field — an author list, a DOI, a numeric result — could not be checked because the publisher blocked automated access (Wiley, SAGE, ScienceDirect and ACS were the usual culprits). Treat those as leads to retrieve through your university's DELNET or INFLIBNET N-LIST access, not as citable facts.

Two claims deserve your own verification before you act on them. The CDSCO screening carve-out is quoted from the guidance PDF itself; the document number and its treatment of monitoring were independently corroborated, but read the PDF yourself before rewriting your positioning on it. And the report that CDSCO classifies a "gait analysis system" as Class A is single-sourced from a consultancy blog — if true it changes your entire regulatory cost, which is exactly why you should confirm it with the Central Licensing Authority rather than trust it.

One search — the clinical evidence base for plantar pressure thresholds and ulceration risk — was cut short by a rate limit. Its core finding survived through other clusters (200 kPa in-shoe is the threshold with consensus), but a dedicated pass on pressure-threshold epidemiology, and on diabetic foot ulcer prevalence in India specifically, is still owed.
